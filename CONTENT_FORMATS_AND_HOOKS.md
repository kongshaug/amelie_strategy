# Content Formats And Hooks

## Purpose And Guardrails

This is a follower-growth production guide for Amelie Anders, not a booking plan. Its job is to turn Amelie's abstract linework, dancing figures, Wabbelis, body-aware placement, artistic process, and on-camera perspective into repeatable content for a global audience.

- Publish in English first. German can be tested as a second version or subtitle layer. Danish is not required.
- Keep the subject anchored in tattooing, art, and authentic creative process. Do not force fashion, relationships, or generic Copenhagen lifestyle into the feed.
- Treat Copenhagen as part of Amelie's story and collaboration network, not as the audience limit.
- Make follows, shares, saves, comments, and repeat viewing the primary calls to action. Mention availability only when relevant and keep booking CTAs secondary.
- Work within 6-10 hours per week. Reuse one filming session across Reels, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, carousels, and Stories.
- Film clients, their stories, or reactions only with informed consent.
- The two face-led unclaimed-design Reels produced 332 and 181 attributed follows, but both were likely boosted. Their organic and paid results are unknown. The format is a strong paid-assisted candidate that requires unboosted organic validation, not a proven organic winner.
- Recent posts/carousels produced more aggregate views and interactions than Reels, although output volume by format is unknown. Design sheets, placement ideas, and strong body-placement images therefore remain central.
- Ordinary documented Reels reached many non-followers but generally converted few follows. Every Reel below gives a reason to follow beyond showing a finished tattoo.
- "Expected growth potential" is a test priority, not a forecast. High means the format combines account-specific evidence or a strong fit with Amelie's moat; medium means it is strategically useful but less validated; experimental means the upside is plausible and must be learned through testing.

## Repeatable Formats

### 1. The Unclaimed Original

- **Name:** The Unclaimed Original
- **Concept:** Amelie appears on camera and creates tension around several strong one-off designs that remain unclaimed. This is the direct organic test of the paid-assisted face-led candidate.
- **Hook:** "I still cannot believe nobody chose these three drawings."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Amelie's face fills the frame beside the strongest design, with `HOW IS THIS STILL UNCLAIMED?` on screen.
- **Structure:** Face and tension line; flash three designs for 0.5-1 second each; show one body-placement mockup per design; give one sentence on the artistic idea; close on all three and a follow prompt.
- **Concrete example:** Feature one dancing figure, one abstract movement line, and one Wabbeli, then place each digitally on a different body area.
- **Caption:** "Three one-off pieces I keep returning to. Which one should become a tattoo first: 1, 2, or 3?"
- **CTA:** "Follow to see which one finds its body, and comment 1, 2, or 3."
- **Why shared:** A viewer can send a specific design to the friend whose style or body placement it fits.
- **Why followed:** The unresolved outcome creates a reason to return, while Amelie's face connects the work to a recognizable artist.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes if placement mockups already exist.
- **Expected growth potential:** High but unvalidated organically. Publish two matched organic replications and a third if the results are mixed. Leave each unboosted for the same seven-day comparison window before using paid support. Compare non-follower reach, profile visits, follows per 1,000 views, and shares with ordinary Reels.

### 2. Movement Becomes A Line

- **Name:** Movement Becomes A Line
- **Concept:** Translate a real human gesture into one of Amelie's abstract dancing figures.
- **Hook:** "This two-second movement became a tattoo drawing."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show the movement and its finished line side by side before any explanation.
- **Structure:** Full movement; freeze the key silhouette; overlay the first traced gesture; show two drawing decisions; reveal the final design; return to movement and line together.
- **Concrete example:** Film Amelie extending one arm and turning her torso, then reduce that gesture into a soft dancing figure.
- **Caption:** "I do not copy the outline. I look for the part of the movement that still feels alive when everything else is removed."
- **CTA:** "Follow for more movement-to-line studies."
- **Why shared:** Dancers, artists, and visually minded viewers can send it as a satisfying translation of motion into art.
- **Why followed:** It makes Amelie's signature method legible and promises an ongoing series rather than a single portfolio image.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-75 minutes including drawing and edit.
- **Expected growth potential:** High; it expresses the clearest distinctive creative process identified in Amelie's positioning, but needs series-level testing.

### 3. One Movement, Three Lines

- **Name:** One Movement, Three Lines
- **Concept:** Show how the same gesture can produce three different original interpretations.
- **Hook:** "One pose does not have one correct line."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Put the source pose in the center and snap three visibly different drawings around it.
- **Structure:** Pose; version A with one decision; version B with one decision; version C with one decision; rapid comparison; Amelie's preference and why.
- **Concrete example:** Turn a seated curled pose into a minimal figure, a loose continuous line, and a Wabbeli-like abstract form without claiming they are interchangeable styles.
- **Caption:** "Same movement, three different choices. I would keep the softness of number 2."
- **CTA:** "Which interpretation holds the movement best: A, B, or C?"
- **Why shared:** The clear choice invites friends to compare taste.
- **Why followed:** Viewers learn how Amelie sees rather than only what she makes.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-60 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; strong comment and repeat-series potential, with follower conversion still to be validated.

### 4. Wabbeli Field Notes

- **Name:** Wabbeli Field Notes
- **Concept:** Make the recurring Wabbeli world understandable one character, shape, or design decision at a time.
- **Hook:** "This is a Wabbeli, but this one needed a completely different kind of wobble."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show the finished Wabbeli large, then cut to Amelie saying its single defining trait.
- **Structure:** Finished form; one verified sentence about the Wabbelis or this design; sketch progression; detail close-up; final design or tattoo; numbered series end card.
- **Concrete example:** Compare three thumbnail shapes and explain why only one had the softness and imbalance Amelie wanted for that Wabbeli.
- **Caption:** "Wabbeli field note 01: the shape can be simple, but it cannot feel still."
- **CTA:** "Follow for the next Wabbeli field note."
- **Why shared:** A named, recognizable visual world gives viewers an object they can identify with and send to others.
- **Why followed:** A numbered world-building series promises continuity and makes Amelie's work easier to recognize.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes using existing sketches.
- **Expected growth potential:** High as a signature-series hypothesis; Wabbelis already recur in Amelie's content, but their follower conversion is not yet proven.

### 5. Which Wabbeli Are You?

- **Name:** Which Wabbeli Are You?
- **Concept:** Present distinct Wabbelis as visual personalities without attaching unsupported psychological claims.
- **Hook:** "Choose a Wabbeli before I tell you which one I would choose."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show four numbered Wabbelis at once with a one-second countdown.
- **Structure:** Four designs; one visual descriptor for each such as quiet, stretched, curled, or restless; Amelie's choice; ask viewers to choose.
- **Concrete example:** A carousel opens with four numbered forms, then gives each a full slide and ends with all four as placement mockups.
- **Caption:** "No personality test, just instinct. Which shape did your eye choose first?"
- **CTA:** "Send this to someone and guess their choice before they answer."
- **Why shared:** It turns specific artwork into a low-friction social choice between friends.
- **Why followed:** Recurring Wabbeli selections build familiarity with Amelie's visual language.
- **Difficulty:** Low; 20-35 minutes with prepared artwork.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; designed for shares and comments, but not supported by current conversion data.

### 6. The Wabbeli Evolution

- **Name:** The Wabbeli Evolution
- **Concept:** Reveal how one Wabbeli changes from first imperfect mark to final one-off form.
- **Hook:** "The first version was too balanced, so I broke it."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Flash the rejected sketch and final form with the changed area circled.
- **Structure:** First sketch; identify the visual problem; erase, stretch, or redraw; show two intermediate states; final piece; optional tattoo reveal.
- **Concrete example:** Show how widening one curve and offsetting the base made a static Wabbeli feel as though it was moving.
- **Caption:** "The cleanest version was not the right one. The small imbalance gave it movement."
- **CTA:** "Save this if you like seeing the decisions behind simple-looking art."
- **Why shared:** The visible improvement is satisfying and useful to other artists.
- **Why followed:** It demonstrates judgment and gives access to a process that finished-work posts hide.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-60 minutes if progress is captured while drawing.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; good fit for art and creative-process audiences.

### 7. Placement Changes The Drawing

- **Name:** Placement Changes The Drawing
- **Concept:** Demonstrate that Amelie's work is designed with the body rather than pasted onto it.
- **Hook:** "This line works on the ribs, but fails on the arm."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Cut between the same design on two placements, mark one `works` and one `fights the body`.
- **Structure:** Design alone; placement A; explain the body line it follows; placement B; show what clashes; adapt the design; final comparison.
- **Concrete example:** Test one long dancing figure along a forearm and side rib, then redraw its curve for each area.
- **Caption:** "Placement is not the last decision. It changes the drawing itself."
- **CTA:** "Follow for more body-aware design breakdowns."
- **Why shared:** It helps tattoo-interested viewers explain a placement question to a friend.
- **Why followed:** It positions Amelie as an artist with a specific body-led method, not merely a source of flash.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-75 minutes with mockups.
- **Expected growth potential:** High; placement content is already among Amelie's stronger visible post concepts, though this exact format is untested.

### 8. Same Design, Three Placements

- **Name:** Same Design, Three Placements
- **Concept:** A saveable carousel comparing how one design reads on three body areas.
- **Hook:** "The same drawing becomes three different tattoos depending on where it lives."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Slide 1 or Reel frame 1 shows all three placements side by side.
- **Structure:** Overview; placement 1 and effect; placement 2 and effect; placement 3 and effect; Amelie's recommendation; clean final comparison.
- **Concrete example:** Show an abstract line on the shoulder blade, side torso, and outer thigh, noting flow rather than declaring one universally best.
- **Caption:** "Shoulder blade, ribs, or thigh? I would adjust the curve differently for every body."
- **CTA:** "Save this for placement ideas and comment your first choice."
- **Why shared:** It is practical inspiration that can be sent during tattoo planning.
- **Why followed:** Viewers expect more useful placement studies in Amelie's recognizable style.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** High for saves and profile visits based on account-specific strength in placement/design carousels; follow lift must still be measured.

### 9. Design Sheet To Body Map

- **Name:** Design Sheet To Body Map
- **Concept:** Pair an available one-off design sheet with specific placement possibilities, while keeping reservation language secondary.
- **Hook:** "I drew these as a family; here is where each line could move best."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show the full design sheet, then rapidly map each numbered design onto a body silhouette.
- **Structure:** Sheet overview; three or four numbered designs; one placement idea each; one alternate placement; final numbered grid.
- **Concrete example:** Map a compact Wabbeli to an ankle, a long movement line to the side torso, and a dancing figure to the upper arm.
- **Caption:** "A design can stay one-off while its placement remains a conversation. My first instincts: 1 ankle, 2 ribs, 3 upper arm."
- **CTA:** "Which pairing should I develop into a full placement study?"
- **Why shared:** It combines inspiration with a simple numbered discussion.
- **Why followed:** It shows both Amelie's art and how she thinks about wearable composition.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-60 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** High; closely related design-and-placement content generated 47 attributed follows in one visible carousel, although paid status is unconfirmed.

### 10. Follow The Body Line

- **Name:** Follow The Body Line
- **Concept:** Use motion to reveal why a finished tattoo belongs in its exact placement.
- **Hook:** "Watch this line change when the body moves."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Start on the healed or finished tattoo in motion, not a static studio setup.
- **Structure:** Movement reveal; freeze on alignment; trace the body line on screen; show still close-up; return to movement from another angle.
- **Concrete example:** Film an arm lifting so a dancing figure appears to extend with the shoulder and upper-arm line.
- **Caption:** "I wanted the drawing to make sense both still and in motion."
- **CTA:** "Send this to someone who notices placement as much as the design."
- **Why shared:** The movement makes a familiar tattoo reveal more surprising and visually satisfying.
- **Why followed:** It repeatedly proves Amelie's promise of drawings that move with the body.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 20-40 minutes during an existing session, with consent.
- **Expected growth potential:** High as a signature visual reveal; exact conversion is untested.

### 11. From Messy Mark To Final Line

- **Name:** From Messy Mark To Final Line
- **Concept:** Compress the full creative refinement from loose marks to a clean original design.
- **Hook:** "This finished line started as the ugliest mark on the page."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show the rough mark and final design in an immediate before-and-after.
- **Structure:** Rough page; isolate one mark; redraw; remove elements; final paper design; optional stencil or tattoo.
- **Concrete example:** Pull one curved gesture from a page of charcoal or pen experiments and refine it into an abstract linework piece.
- **Caption:** "The useful part of a rough sketch is often the part I almost ignore."
- **CTA:** "Follow if you want the unfinished pages too, not only final tattoos."
- **Why shared:** Artists and creative viewers relate to unattractive early work becoming resolved.
- **Why followed:** It broadens Amelie from tattoo portfolio to creative-process creator without leaving an authentic topic.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-75 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** High for adjacent art audiences; the format itself has not been validated on Amelie's account.

### 12. One Line, Three Decisions

- **Name:** One Line, Three Decisions
- **Concept:** Explain three non-obvious decisions inside a deceptively simple linework design.
- **Hook:** "This looks like one simple line, but these three choices changed everything."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show the final piece with three points appearing on screen.
- **Structure:** Final overview; decision 1 on proportion; decision 2 on negative space; decision 3 on movement or placement; full reveal.
- **Concrete example:** Explain why the head stays small, one side remains open, and the lower curve follows the hip rather than closing the figure.
- **Caption:** "Simple is not the absence of decisions. It is deciding what can disappear."
- **CTA:** "Save this for the next time a simple drawing looks effortless."
- **Why shared:** It gives art students and tattoo viewers a concise way to see design more closely.
- **Why followed:** Amelie becomes a guide to visual thinking, not only the maker of an isolated result.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes using existing work.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; strong authority and save potential, with unknown follow conversion.

### 13. The Redraw

- **Name:** The Redraw
- **Concept:** Put a rejected and accepted version side by side and explain one precise reason for changing it.
- **Hook:** "I deleted the prettiest part because it stopped the movement."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Cross out the rejected element over the finished comparison.
- **Structure:** Version A; identify the issue; live redraw or replay; version B; side-by-side test on a body mockup.
- **Concrete example:** Remove an elegant extra loop because it competes with the torso curve of a dancing figure.
- **Caption:** "A detail can be beautiful alone and still be wrong for the whole drawing."
- **CTA:** "A or B before the explanation? Comment honestly."
- **Why shared:** The counterintuitive removal sparks debate among visual and creative audiences.
- **Why followed:** Viewers return for Amelie's taste and reasoning, not just outcomes.
- **Difficulty:** Low; 25-40 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; repeatable opinion plus process mechanism.

### 14. Draw Under One Constraint

- **Name:** Draw Under One Constraint
- **Concept:** Give Amelie a narrow artistic rule and show the result without turning it into a gimmick unrelated to her practice.
- **Hook:** "Can I keep the movement if I never lift the pen?"
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Pen touches paper beside the written rule `ONE CONTINUOUS LINE`.
- **Structure:** State constraint; first attempt; one failure; second attempt; final design; brief judgment on whether the constraint helped.
- **Concrete example:** Draw a dancing figure in one continuous line, then compare it with Amelie's normal process.
- **Caption:** "The rule forced a softer connection here, but I would still redraw the ending."
- **CTA:** "Give me the next drawing constraint, but keep it about line or movement."
- **Why shared:** A clear challenge creates suspense and a satisfying resolution.
- **Why followed:** Audience-supplied constraints create an ongoing participatory art series.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-60 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; strong short-form mechanism but no account-specific validation.

### 15. Tattoo Day In 15 Seconds

- **Name:** Tattoo Day In 15 Seconds
- **Concept:** A compressed process story centered on the design's transformation, not a generic day-in-the-life vlog.
- **Hook:** "Fifteen seconds from paper movement to a line living on the body."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Final tattoo flashes first, then hard cut back to the sketch.
- **Structure:** Final preview; drawing; placement discussion; stencil; one clean non-graphic process detail; wipe; reveal in movement.
- **Concrete example:** Follow a small dancing figure from last sketch adjustment through upper-arm placement and final moving reveal.
- **Caption:** "The shortest version of a process that depends on many small decisions."
- **CTA:** "Follow for the longer design breakdown behind the next one."
- **Why shared:** It delivers a fast, complete transformation with a strong visual payoff.
- **Why followed:** The end promises deeper future explanations instead of treating the process as disposable spectacle.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-75 minutes of capture during a session plus edit.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium; process is highly reusable, but Amelie's documented little tattoo vlog converted only 3 follows from 3,929 views, so this must be sharper and outcome-first.

### 16. The Quiet Process Loop

- **Name:** The Quiet Process Loop
- **Concept:** A short tactile sequence of drawing, stencil, gloves, wiping, and final line that loops seamlessly and stays non-graphic.
- **Hook:** "The calmest 8 seconds of making a moving line."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Begin with the most precise satisfying line or wipe and title the process immediately.
- **Structure:** Three or four close-up actions; final tattoo; match the final curve to the opening drawing curve for a loop.
- **Concrete example:** Match a pen tracing a Wabbeli curve to the same curve being revealed on skin.
- **Caption:** "Paper, placement, line, movement."
- **CTA:** "Save this for a quiet creative reset."
- **Why shared:** The compact sensory loop can be sent for its calming visual rhythm.
- **Why followed:** Consistent process aesthetics make Amelie's hand and visual world recognizable over time.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes with a reusable edit template.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium; useful high-probability production format, but not expected to convert as strongly as narrative or tension-led formats.

### 17. Reply With A Drawing

- **Name:** Reply With A Drawing
- **Concept:** Answer a real comment by drawing or demonstrating the response on screen.
- **Hook:** Use the exact comment, for example: "Would this still work on a shoulder?"
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Place the comment above Amelie's face or hand, then immediately reveal a shoulder mockup.
- **Structure:** Comment; direct answer; visual demonstration; one caveat; finished option; invite the next question.
- **Concrete example:** Reply to a placement question by adapting the same long abstract line to the shoulder blade and explaining the changed curve.
- **Caption:** "Replying with the drawing because placement is easier to show than describe."
- **CTA:** "Leave the next question about line, movement, healing, or placement."
- **Why shared:** The answer is useful to people with the same specific question.
- **Why followed:** Viewers see that following and commenting can shape future content.
- **Difficulty:** Low; 20-40 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; strong community and search-language potential, but untested for Amelie.

### 18. Amelie's Linework Opinion

- **Name:** Amelie's Linework Opinion
- **Concept:** State a clear, defensible artistic opinion and prove it with Amelie's own work rather than attacking other artists or clients.
- **Hook:** "A tattoo does not need more detail. Sometimes it needs a better empty space."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Amelie says the opinion to camera while two of her designs appear beside her.
- **Structure:** Opinion; weak version made by Amelie for demonstration; improved version; visual reason; concise conclusion.
- **Concrete example:** Compare an overfilled study with a version that leaves the figure open so the body completes the composition.
- **Caption:** "My preference: let the body and the empty space do part of the drawing."
- **CTA:** "Follow for opinions I can demonstrate, not rules everyone must follow."
- **Why shared:** A precise, non-hostile stance gives viewers language for their own aesthetic preference.
- **Why followed:** Perspective makes Amelie memorable as a person and artist, not just another portfolio account.
- **Difficulty:** Low to medium; 30-45 minutes.
- **Expected growth potential:** High as a personality and authority test; opinions are currently underrepresented in the available account data.

### 19. Not A Rule, A Tradeoff

- **Name:** Not A Rule, A Tradeoff
- **Concept:** Replace simplistic tattoo advice with a visual explanation of an actual artistic tradeoff.
- **Hook:** "Small is not automatically delicate; here is the tradeoff I look at."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Show two versions at different scales with `NOT A RULE` and `A TRADEOFF` labels.
- **Structure:** Common oversimplification; two visual options; what each gains; what each loses; Amelie's preferred question to ask.
- **Concrete example:** Compare a compact dancing figure with a larger version and discuss readable negative space without making unsupported healing guarantees.
- **Caption:** "I would rather show the tradeoff than pretend one size is right for every drawing and body."
- **CTA:** "Save this question for your next design conversation: what should remain readable?"
- **Why shared:** Nuance is useful to tattoo-curious viewers who encounter absolute advice online.
- **Why followed:** It builds trust without relying on sensational myths or universal claims.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 40-60 minutes and careful wording.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; authority and save potential, with lower entertainment upside than transformations.

### 20. Healed And Moving

- **Name:** Healed And Moving
- **Concept:** Show a healed Amelie tattoo in body movement, with date or healing interval included only when verified.
- **Hook:** "This is how the line lives on the body after healing."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Open on the healed tattoo moving naturally, labeled `HEALED`.
- **Structure:** Moving reveal; still close-up; original drawing; fresh image if available; healed movement again; one factual reflection.
- **Concrete example:** Revisit a healed dancing figure and show how its curve reads when the client bends and extends the arm.
- **Caption:** "Healed work matters because the tattoo is made to live beyond the reveal. Thank you to the client for returning and allowing me to share it."
- **CTA:** "Follow for more healed work, not only fresh tattoos."
- **Why shared:** It answers a practical viewer interest while preserving the visual movement central to Amelie's work.
- **Why followed:** Repeated healed evidence shows long-term attention to the work and creates anticipation for follow-ups.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; filming is simple, but arranging a consented return visit adds coordination.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium; healed carousels in the visible ranking drew views and interactions but few or no visible attributed follows, so a movement-led opening and explicit follow promise are necessary.

### 21. Fresh Versus Healed, Same Motion

- **Name:** Fresh Versus Healed, Same Motion
- **Concept:** Match the same camera angle and body movement when fresh and healed for a direct visual comparison.
- **Hook:** "Same line, same movement, fresh and healed."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Split screen the matched fresh and healed frames immediately.
- **Structure:** Split-screen motion; fresh close-up; healed close-up; original design; concise observation limited to what can be seen.
- **Concrete example:** Match an upper-arm Wabbeli at reveal and at a later consented healed check, without claiming a general healing outcome.
- **Caption:** "A matched view of this piece fresh and healed. This shows one tattoo, not a promise about every body or healing process."
- **CTA:** "Save this comparison and follow for future healed checks."
- **Why shared:** The exact matched comparison is more useful than unrelated before-and-after images.
- **Why followed:** It creates a longitudinal series people can return to.
- **Difficulty:** Medium to high; requires planning, consent, and matched footage over time.
- **Expected growth potential:** Medium to high; strong proof format, but dependent on access to healed work.

- **Why followed:** The open-ended collection creates narrative continuity around Amelie's client collaborations.
- **Difficulty:** Medium; 45-75 minutes plus consent.

### 23. Friendship Wabbelis, Not Matching Copies

### 24. One Prompt, Two Artists

- **Name:** One Prompt, Two Artists
- **Concept:** Amelie and another tattoo artist or visual artist independently interpret one movement-based prompt, then reveal both results in a Collab post.
- **Hook:** "We drew the same movement without seeing each other's page."
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Both artists reveal their drawings at the same time in a split screen.
- **Structure:** Shared prompt; each artist's first marks; simultaneous reveal; one observation from each; final side-by-side and collaborator credit.
- **Concrete example:** Amelie turns a turning torso into abstract linework while a Copenhagen illustrator interprets the same clip in their own established medium.
- **Caption:** "One movement, two visual languages. Created with [collaborator] using the same reference and no shared sketch."
- **CTA:** "Follow both artists for the reverse prompt next."
- **Why shared:** The contrast invites comparison and exposes each creator to the other's audience.
- **Why followed:** The promised reverse prompt provides continuity, while the collaboration introduces Amelie's distinct visual language in context.
- **Difficulty:** High; 1.5-3 hours including coordination, but each artist can film separately.
- **Expected growth potential:** High upside, experimental. Existing studio Collabs concentrated more Danish viewers but converted only 2-3 visible follows, so choose concept-led audience overlap rather than a generic tattoo-day Collab.

### 25. A Line Through Another Medium

- **Name:** A Line Through Another Medium
- **Concept:** Collaborate with an artist in an adjacent medium to translate one Amelie drawing while keeping the topic firmly about art and process.
- **Hook:** "What happens when a tattoo line leaves skin and enters another material?"
- **First 1-3 seconds:** Match-cut Amelie's drawn line into the collaborator's material.
- **Structure:** Original line; collaborator's material and first action; two translation decisions; finished object; original and translation together; Collab credit.
- **Concrete example:** A ceramicist, textile artist, photographer, or animator translates the movement of one Wabbeli or abstract line in their established medium.
- **Caption:** "I gave [collaborator] a line; they decided what movement meant in [medium]."
- **CTA:** "Which authentic medium should the next line enter?"
- **Why shared:** Cross-medium transformation is novel to both audiences and naturally showcases the collaborator.
- **Why followed:** It positions Amelie as a contemporary visual artist whose language can generate a wider creative world.
- **Difficulty:** High; 2-4 hours across coordination and edit, so limit to one per month.
- **Expected growth potential:** High upside, experimental; no account-specific evidence yet supports a particular adjacent medium or partner.

### 26. The Drawing That Moved Countries

- **Name:** The Drawing That Moved Countries
- **Concept:** Use Amelie's verified German-to-Copenhagen transition as a frame for changes in her creative practice, without manufacturing lifestyle content or dramatic claims.

## Filming And Editing Standard

1. **Capture vertically:** Film 9:16 at 1080 x 1920 or higher. Keep faces, text, and the tattoo inside safe central areas so interface elements do not cover them.
2. **Show the payoff first:** In the first 1-3 seconds, use the finished line, body movement, before-and-after, face-led tension, or side-by-side choice. Do not open on studio doors, coffee, equipment setup, or walking footage.
3. **Make the premise readable without sound:** Put one short English hook on the first frame and add accurate English captions to speech. Keep text high contrast and large enough for a phone screen.
4. **Use English-first audio:** Amelie can speak naturally in English. If a German version is tested, publish it as a deliberate separate test or clearly subtitled version rather than mixing languages without a reason.
5. **Keep one idea per post:** A Reel should resolve one question, transformation, opinion, or comparison. Default to 8-20 seconds for visual ideas and 20-35 seconds only when the explanation earns the extra time. These are production defaults, not algorithm claims.
6. **Cut on information:** Change the frame when a new design, decision, placement, or process stage appears. Remove greetings, repeated context, and endings after the payoff.
7. **Record a reusable session shot list:** Capture a two-second face intro, source movement, sketch wide shot, pen close-up, placement discussion, stencil, one clean non-graphic process detail, still reveal, moving reveal, and one client-approved vertical photo.
8. **Prioritize clean art and process:** Avoid graphic close-ups. Show the line, hand, stencil, wipe, and final movement rather than making the needle the main event.
9. **Build two edit templates:** Template A is face or comment, three proof cuts, follow promise. Template B is outcome, process stages, moving reveal. Templates save time without making every concept identical.
10. **Export a clean master:** Save without a platform watermark, then post natively to Instagram Reels, TikTok, and YouTube Shorts. Adapt the caption and cover, not the core edit.
11. **Create carousel assets while filming:** Save the design sheet, rejected version, placement mockups, fresh still, and healed still. One Reel should often produce one later carousel rather than another filming session.
12. **Use original or licensed sound:** Voice, room sound, pen sound, and licensed platform audio are enough. Do not force a trend that obscures Amelie's work or perspective.
13. **Protect consent:** Agree before filming on whether the body, face, voice, story, and healed follow-up can be shown. A content idea is never more important than client privacy.
14. **Judge series, not anecdotes:** Judge a format after at least three executions or 10,000 combined views, whichever comes first, where access and safety allow. Record non-follower reach, average watch time, completion rate when available, shares, saves, profile visits, follows, and follows per 1,000 views. Compare results at equal post age, and evaluate paid and organic distribution separately.
